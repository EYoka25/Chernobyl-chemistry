I
ACT ONE
Soviet Ukraine — Spring 1986

Pripyat was a Soviet planned city built in the 1970s to house workers and their families for the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Station. It was a model of Soviet progress — new apartment blocks, a cultural centre, a sports complex, a hospital. The city had been designed to accommodate 50,000 residents. By April 1986, it held 49,360.

The city was prosperous by Soviet standards. Workers at the nuclear plant were among the most privileged in the USSR — the system had been designed to attract the best engineers, physicists, and technicians. Fully inserting all rods stops the chain reaction. Withdrawing them allows it. The RBMK used 211 control rods. On the night of April 26, only 6–8 were inserted — well below the regulatory minimum of 15. The reactor was, in effect, operating without its primary safety mechanism.

Families had built lives there. Children attended schools. Women worked in the administrative offices and medical facilities. The city had a newspaper, a cinema, a marketplace. In the spring of 1986, the city was preparing for May Day celebrations — a national holiday. The Ferris wheel was being assembled in the central square. It was scheduled to open on May 1. It would never operate.

Why Pripyat?

The Soviet Union needed electricity. Nuclear power was the future — the peaceful atom, harnessed for the workers' state. The RBMK-1000 reactor was a Soviet design, developed in the 1960s. It was powerful, it was Soviet, and it was cheaper to build than Western alternatives. That it had design flaws that made catastrophic failure structurally inevitable was a fact known to Soviet engineers and suppressed as a state secret.

Pripyat was built in a place to live. Workers received higher-than-average wages, access to goods that were scarce elsewhere in the USSR, and apartment blocks that were modern and well-maintained. Many families had been there for a decade. Their children had been born there. They had no plans to leave.

The Test That Would Never End

On the night of April 25–26, a safety test was scheduled for Reactor No. 4. The test — the fourth attempt over three years — was designed to determine whether the coasting turbines, after a reactor shutdown, could generate enough electricity to power emergency cooling pumps during the 75-second gap before backup diesel generators came online.

It was an administrative checkbox, a bureaucratic sign-off. It was the last item required before the plant's current operating licence could be formally renewed. Reactor operators had been briefed only briefly. The night shift, which would conduct the test, had not participated in the preparation. None of this was considered a problem.

II
ACT TWO
Unit 4 Control Room — April 26, 1986

At 01:05 AM on April 26, 1986, Deputy Chief Engineer Dyatlov gave the order that would seal the fate of Unit 4.

The reactor was running at 200 MW thermal — far below the required 700 MW minimum for the test. The xenon poisoning from the nine-hour delay had suppressed the chain reaction. To reach even this meagre power level had taken extraordinary effort and had required withdrawing almost all available control rods. By regulation, a minimum of 15 rods must remain inserted at all times as a safety buffer. That night, only 6 to 8 remained.

◉ DECISION POINT 2 OF 4 01:05 — April 26, 1986

Dyatlov has ordered the Emergency Core Cooling System (ECCS) to be disconnected. He argues it will interfere with the turbine test readings. The ECCS is the automated system that floods the reactor core with coolant water in an emergency. Without it, if the core begins to overheat, there is no automatic response.

This requires your sign-off as Senior Reactor Control Engineer. You are Leonid Toptunov. You have been on shift for one hour. The instruments show the reactor is barely stable.

Dyatlov is standing behind you. He is waiting for your acknowledgement. What do you do?

III
ACT THREE
Soviet denial and the first responders

In the hours after the explosion, Soviet authorities did what the system had been designed to do: they managed the information.

Plant Director Viktor Bryukhanov sent a telex to Moscow stating that radiation levels at the site were 3.6 röntgens per hour. This was the maximum reading his instruments could display — because all instruments capable of measuring higher levels had been found broken or had been put away. The actual radiation in some areas exceeded 15,000 röntgens per hour, a lethal dose in minutes.

What actually happened — and why it was allowed to
The RBMK-1000 core
THE SETUP

The Xenon Trap

Xenon-135 is a fission byproduct that absorbs neutrons. After a reactor has been running at high power for an extended period, xenon accumulates in the fuel. When power is reduced or the reactor is shut down, xenon levels spike dramatically — a phenomenon called xenon poisoning. The reactor becomes extremely difficult to control.

The nine-hour delay in starting the test meant the reactor would be severely xenon-poisoned by the time the test began. The test required power levels of 700–1,600 MW. Reaching those levels would be nearly impossible. The operators would have to withdraw almost all control rods to compensate.

THE CRITICAL FAILURE

Control Rod Depletion

Control rods are the primary safety mechanism of a nuclear reactor. Inserting them stops the chain reaction. Fully inserting all rods stops the chain reaction. Withdrawing them allows it. The RBMK used 211 control rods. On the night of April 26, only 6–8 were inserted — well below the regulatory minimum of 15. The reactor was, in effect, operating without its primary safety mechanism.

THE FATAL FLAW

Positive Void Coefficient

In virtually all Western reactor designs, if coolant water boils away and turns to steam, the chain reaction slows automatically — because water acts as both coolant and moderator. Lose the water, lose the moderation, lose the reaction. This is an inherent physical safety property.

The RBMK worked differently. Its moderator was graphite — a solid that was unaffected by whether water was present. If coolant flashed to steam, it stopped absorbing neutrons — but the graphite kept moderating. The result: more steam meant more reactivity. In nuclear engineering, this is called a positive void coefficient, and it meant that if something went wrong, the reactor would tend to make it worse. This flaw was documented internally. It was suppressed. The operators who ran the reactor every day did not know it existed.

THE KILLING BLOW

The Graphite Tip Effect

The RBMK's control rods had a second, catastrophic flaw that was known to Soviet engineers and never corrected. Each rod had a graphite tip at its lower end — designed to displace water and improve fuel efficiency when rods were fully withdrawn.

When the AZ-5 emergency shutdown was pressed at 01:23:40, all 211 rods began descending simultaneously. For the first 2–3 seconds, the graphite tips entered the core ahead of the neutron-absorbing boron section. For those seconds, instead of slowing the chain reaction, the rods were accelerating it.

In a reactor already running at approximately 30,000 MW thermal — nearly ten times its design maximum — that two-second spike was enough. The fuel fragmented in nanoseconds. Steam pressure shattered the reactor. The 1,000-tonne upper biological shield was lifted from its mountings by the force of the explosion and fell back askew. The reactor was open to the atmosphere. It was over.

The Zone, the land, the long count of the dead

The Chernobyl explosion released between 5% and 60% of the reactor's radioactive inventory into the atmosphere over the course of ten days — a plume that drifted across the Soviet Union, Eastern Europe, and Scandinavia.

The Red Forest

The pine forest immediately northwest of the reactor — approximately 4 km² — turned rust-red and died within days as trees absorbed lethal radiation doses. Workers called it the Red Forest. The dead trees were bulldozed and buried in trenches within the exclusion zone — trenches that remain radioactive. The forest has partially regrown, but the soil remains among the most contaminated on Earth.

The Elephant's Foot

In the flooded basement of the reactor building, molten fuel and sand mixed together and cooled into a solid mass shaped like an elephant's foot. It is one of the most radioactive objects on Earth — so intensely radioactive that a person standing 3 metres away would receive a lethal dose in minutes. It remains in the basement today, behind the New Safe Confinement, still emitting radiation.

The Long Count

💀

Immediate Deaths

Two workers died in the immediate explosion. Twenty-eight more died of acute radiation syndrome in the following weeks and months. The names of all thirty are known. They are not forgotten.

🏥

Long-Term Health Effects

Estimates of excess cancer deaths attributable to Chernobyl range from 4,000 to 6,000 over the lifetime of exposed populations. The range is wide because radiation epidemiology is uncertain, and because the Soviet Union did not keep comprehensive health records. The true number will never be known with certainty.

👥

The Liquidators

Between 1986 and 1990, approximately 600,000 workers — called liquidators — were deployed to contain the disaster. They cleaned up contaminated areas, built the sarcophagus around the reactor, and decontaminated surrounding land. Many received high radiation doses. Many have since died of radiation-related illnesses. Their sacrifice is documented but often forgotten in the West.

🗺

Chernobyl Today

The Exclusion Zone has been open to guided visitors since the 1990s. After the 2019 HBO miniseries, tourist visits increased by an estimated 30–40%. Visitors walk the silent streets of Pripyat, pass the Ferris wheel that never operated (it was assembled days before the explosion, for a May Day celebration that never came), and stand before the New Safe Confinement. In 2022, Russian forces occupied the Exclusion Zone for thirty-five days during the invasion of Ukraine, potentially dispersing radioactive material from disturbed soil.

"The Chernobyl disaster was not merely a failure of technology. It was the failure of a system — of secrecy, of the suppression of truth, of the belief that admitting error was more dangerous than the error itself. The system was its own worst enemy."
— Valery Legasov, recorded memoirs, April 1988. Found after his death.
VI
ACT SIX
What we learned, what we forgot, what remains

Chernobyl was not an aberration. It was the inevitable consequence of a system that prioritized secrecy over safety, that suppressed technical truth in service of political narrative, and that believed that admitting error was more dangerous than allowing catastrophe.

The RBMK reactor design had known flaws. Those flaws were documented. The documentation was classified. The operators who ran the reactors every day of their working lives did not know these facts. They could not protect themselves or anyone else from dangers they had been prevented from knowing existed.

After Chernobyl

In the years following the disaster, the Soviet Union conducted a comprehensive review of reactor safety. The RBMK reactors that remained in operation were retrofitted with upgraded control systems and additional safety features. The positive void coefficient was mitigated through design changes. The graphite tip flaw was addressed. But these improvements were made only after the catastrophe. The flaws had been known. They had been suppressed.

Today, 11 RBMK reactors remain in operation in Russia and Belarus — the only RBMK reactors still operating anywhere in the world. They are among the oldest continuously operating nuclear reactors on Earth. They are operated with enhanced safety protocols, but the fundamental design flaws remain.

The Zone

The Exclusion Zone — a 30-kilometre radius around the reactor — remains off-limits to permanent human habitation. The soil is contaminated. The groundwater is contaminated. The forest is contaminated. Yet life has returned. Birds nest in the dead trees. Deer graze in the radioactive meadows. In 1998, a herd of Przewalski's wild horses — nearly extinct — was introduced to the zone and has thrived. The finding has prompted serious scientific debate about whether radiation exposure is, for wildlife, less damaging than the human activities it replaced.

It Was Always Going to Happen

You've reached the end. Now the truth: it didn't matter which choices you made. Not because people's choices don't matter — but because the system surrounding those people had made catastrophic failure structurally inevitable.

The RBMK's positive void coefficient was documented and suppressed. The graphite tip flaw was known and uncorrected. These facts were classified as state secrets. The operators who ran the reactor every day of their working lives did not know these things about the machine they operated. They could not protect themselves or anyone else from a danger they had been prevented from knowing existed.

Legasov understood this when he stood before the IAEA in Vienna. He chose to protect the programme over protecting the truth. Then, for two years, he watched what that choice had cost. The cassette tapes were his reckoning.

Leonid Toptunov was 26 years old. He pressed the AZ-5 button because he was told to. He stayed at his post because he was told the reactor was intact. He walked through corridors where the radiation was measured in hours-to-death because no one told him otherwise. He died on May 14, 1986.

The disaster was not the failure of men. It was the failure of the system that shaped them.
